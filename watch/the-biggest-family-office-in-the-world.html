About this film

Who runs the biggest family office in the world? It looks after about $2.3 trillion, owns a small slice of more than seven thousand companies, spends only what it earns, and wrote its rules six years before the first money went in. This film tells whose office it is, how its rules were written before the money arrived, and what any family can take from it when a windfall comes: decide first, write it down, and keep to it.

Introduction

0:04HouseholdIf our family ever came into real money, how would we make sure it’s still there for our grandchildren?

0:11I’m Freya, Finna’s AI chief of staff. Let me tell you about a family. It runs the biggest family office in the world. Not one of the biggest. The biggest. This family’s office looks after about two point three trillion dollars.

0:31That is more than the yearly output of most countries on earth. It owns a small slice of more than seven thousand companies, in nearly seventy countries. Put together, about one and a half percent of the shares of all the world’s listed companies. If you have a pension, your money and theirs very likely sit side by side in the same shares.

0:58The family is large. More than five and a half million members. Divide the office by the family, and each member’s share is worth about four hundred thousand dollars. Children included. Now, here is what makes it unusual. Not the size. The habits.

1:20The family spends only what the office earns over time: about three percent a year. The rest is never touched. It publishes everything it owns, every share in every company, twice a year, for anyone to read. It has written rules about what it will not own, and a council that checks them.

1:44And when markets crashed in 2008, its investments fell by almost a quarter in a single year. It did not sell. It kept to the plan, and kept buying. Where did the money come from? From one find, deep underground, reported just before Christmas, 1969. But here’s the part I find remarkable.

2:10When the family decided how to manage it, it wrote the rules first. The law that created the office was passed in 1990. The first money went in six years later. For six years, the family had a rule book for an account that was still empty. So who is this family?

The family’s name

2:33The family’s name is Norway. Five and a half million people. One office. Its official name is the Government Pension Fund Global. Most Norwegians just call it the oil fund. To be fair to the word, it isn’t a family office in the legal sense.

2:52It’s a sovereign wealth fund: a fund a country owns, here on behalf of its people. Norway’s central bank manages it, and Parliament sets its frame. But look at how it behaves. A long view, one record of everything, written rules, and money kept for people who aren’t born yet.

3:15That is exactly what a family office is for. And the find before Christmas 1969? That was oil, under the North Sea. A field called Ekofisk. Norway was not yet a rich country. Fishing, shipping, forests, a long coast. Oil could have changed it overnight.

3:41And in many countries, sudden oil money has done more harm than good. Economists even have a name for it: the resource curse. So the question Norway asked was not, “How do we spend this?” It was, “How do we make sure this doesn’t spend us?” Two words to keep from this chapter.

4:03A sovereign wealth fund: a fund a country owns, invested for the long term. And the resource curse: when sudden wealth from the ground does a country more harm than good.

Rules before money

4:17Norway’s answer came in steps. Each one is a habit any family could recognise. 1971. Decide who it belongs to. Parliament set out ten principles for the oil, often called the ten oil commandments. The oil would be under national control, and the state would take part directly. Farouk Al-Kasim, an Iraqi-born geologist who had settled in Norway, helped write the plan they came from.

4:461972. Keep a hand on it. Norway set up its own state oil company, Statoil, now called Equinor. A share of every barrel came home. 1990. Write the rules before the money arrives. Parliament passed the law that created the fund. Oil income would flow in. Only what the budget needed would flow out. 1996. The first deposit. Six years after the law.

5:202001. Decide how much you may spend. The fiscal rule: each year, the government may spend about the fund’s expected real return, not the fund itself. First, four percent. In 2017, lowered to three. The capital stays. 2004. Decide what you won’t own.

5:45Ethical guidelines, and a Council on Ethics that recommends which companies to exclude. 2006. Say who it’s for. The fund took its current name, the Government Pension Fund Global. Its name looks ahead to the pensions of a country growing older. One more decision mattered as much as all the others. The fund invests only outside Norway. Why? If all that oil money were spent or invested at home, it would push up wages and prices so fast that everything else Norway makes, its fish, its ships, its factories, would become too expensive to sell.

6:26Investing abroad keeps the windfall from swamping the home economy. It also means Norway isn’t betting its future on Norway alone. And the rules have lasted. Governments of the left and the right have come and gone since 1990. Each has argued about details.

6:45None has broken the core: save the oil, spend only the return. A word to keep from this chapter. A fiscal rule: a written limit on what a government may spend. Here, about the fund’s expected real return.

Five desks, one country

7:05A family office does five jobs. We call them desks. Norway’s office runs all five. Records. Everything in one place, and in the open. The fund lists every company it owns, twice a year. Anyone, anywhere, can look. Policy. Who decides what, written down. Parliament sets the frame. The Ministry of Finance sets the strategy.

7:32The central bank carries it out. The spending rule and the ethics rules sit on top. No one person can change course on a whim. Investment. Broad, simple, and cheap. At the end of 2025, about seventy-one percent was in shares, about twenty-six percent in bonds, and the rest in property and renewable energy.

7:57Mostly it follows the whole market, rather than trying to beat it. In 2025, it returned about fifteen percent. Protection. Never everything in one place. Thousands of companies, dozens of countries, many currencies. When one market falls, others usually don’t fall as far.

8:20It still has bad quarters. It lost almost two percent in the first three months of 2026, then ended the half-year up more than nine. What protects it is the rule that it never has to sell when prices are low. Succession. Built for people who aren’t born yet. The fund’s stated aim is that both today’s Norwegians, and the generations to come, benefit from the oil wealth.

8:47Its returns now pay for about a quarter of the state budget, and the capital is still there for the next generation. And here’s the humility part. In August 2026, the head of the fund, Nicolai Tangen, said it was not improbable that its entire value could one day be lost.

9:08The people who run it best are the ones who take that most seriously. A word to keep from this chapter. Diversification: spreading money across many companies, countries, and kinds of investment, so no single fall can take it all.

The neighbour across the sea

9:27Now, let’s look across the water. The United Kingdom found oil in the same sea, at about the same time, and in comparable amounts. But they made different choices about the money. In the UK, oil taxes went into the general budget, year by year, like any other income. They were spent on the needs of the day.

9:52There was no separate fund, and no rule about what to keep. Norway put the money aside, under rules, in a fund it couldn’t easily raid. How big is the difference? The UK government has estimated it. Had Britain saved its North Sea tax receipts since 1980, and left them to grow untouched, the fund could now be worth about one trillion pounds.

10:21To be fair, it’s not a perfect comparison. Norway also took a direct share of its fields, and owns most of its oil company, so profits came home as well as taxes. Britain licensed its fields mostly to private companies, and taxed them. And Britain had reasons to spend: the money arrived in hard years, and it was used.

10:45In October 2026, the UK announced an Energy Wealth Fund, starting with two billion pounds, to be paid into from North Sea taxes over time. A start, with a sea that is now past its best years. So the lesson isn’t that one country was wise, and the other wasn’t. It’s simpler than that. A windfall without a rule tends to become ordinary income.

11:16And ordinary income gets spent. A word to keep from this chapter. A windfall: a large sum that arrives once, rather than as regular income.

In a family’s office

11:32Most families will never have an oil field. But many will have a windfall at some point. An inheritance. A bonus. The sale of a home, or a business. A pension paid out as a lump sum.

11:50Norway’s habits work at any size. Here they are, at kitchen-table scale. Write the rule before the money arrives. Decide, while you’re calm, what a windfall is for. How much to keep, how much to enjoy, how much to give. A decision made before the money lands is far easier to keep than one made after.

12:16Keep it apart from everyday money. Two jars, not one. Money that sits in the everyday account tends to become everyday spending. Spend from the return, not the capital. Norway’s own rule is about three percent a year. Every family’s number is different, and depends on its age, its needs, and its other income.

12:40The idea is the same: decide what the money may give each year, and leave the rest working. Don’t put everything on what you already depend on. Norway invests abroad because its own economy already runs on oil. A family whose income comes from one company or one industry might think twice about saving in it too. Make it possible not to sell in a bad year.

13:08Norway keeps to its plan in a crash, because nothing forces it to sell. For a family, that usually means a cash buffer, so a falling market never has to pay for the roof. Keep one record, in the open. Everyone who needs to know what the family owns, and where, can see it. Say who it’s for. Norway named its fund for the pensions of the future.

13:33A family can write down who its money is meant to serve, and make sure the next generation knows. None of this needs a trillion dollars. It needs a few decisions, written down, and kept.

In short

13:48Three things to take with you. One. The biggest family office in the world belongs to a country. Norway turned one oil find into a fund for its people, worth about two point three trillion dollars. Two. It isn’t the size that keeps it. It’s the rules: written before the money came, spending only the return, investing broadly, and never forced to sell.

14:15Three. A windfall without a rule tends to become ordinary income. Any family can write its own rule, at its own scale. So, to your question: how do you make sure it’s still there for your grandchildren? Do what Norway did. Decide first, write it down, and keep to it. You’ll find Finna at finnafamilyoffice.com.

14:43Ready when you are.
