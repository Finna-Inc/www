The idea, and the evidence

What is a family office?

A private team whose only client is one family, looking after its whole financial life. The idea is more than a century old, it has never been more popular, and the problems it exists to solve are ones most families share. Every figure on this page links to its source.

See the numbers Read the sources

The short answer

An office with one client: the family.

A family office is a private company that runs the financial and personal affairs of a wealthy family. A single family office serves one family; a multi-family office shares its people across several.

In the United States the term has a legal meaning. Under the SEC’s Family Office Rule, an office advises only family clients, is wholly owned and controlled by the family, and does not offer its services to the public.1

What sets it apart is scope. A bank looks after accounts, an adviser looks after a portfolio and a lawyer looks after a will. The office holds all of it at once, works only for the family and remembers why every decision was made.

A short history

From the king’s steward to Room 5600.

The family office is older than most banks families use today. Six moments in how it became what it is.

  1. 6th centuryThe steward

    The idea is usually traced to the king’s steward, who managed a royal family’s wealth and estates.3

  2. 1882The first modern office

    John D. Rockefeller opens an office in New York to manage his family’s investment, personal and philanthropic interests. It came to be known as Room 5600.4, 5

  3. 1907An office for other families

    Steel magnate Henry Phipps founds Bessemer Trust as his family’s office. In 1974 it takes its first clients from outside the family.6

  4. 1980The office goes on record

    Rockefeller & Co. registers with the SEC as an investment adviser. It goes on to manage money for other wealthy families too.4, 5

  5. 2011Defined in law

    On June 22, 2011, the SEC adopts the Family Office Rule, setting out which offices need not register as investment advisers.1

  6. 2024The boom

    About 8,030 single family offices worldwide, 68% of them founded after 2000.2

The numbers today

A small club, growing fast.

Only about 8,000 families in the world run an office of their own. Their number has grown by almost a third in five years.

  • $3.1 trillionmanaged by single family offices today, expected to reach $5.4 trillion by 2030.2
  • 3,180single family offices in North America, about four in ten of the world’s.2
  • 68%of all single family offices were founded after 2000.2
  • 86%of family offices lack a clear succession plan for their key decision makers.7

Built for the very wealthiest.

  • $100 million+in investable assets: the families that family offices generally serve, industry commenters told the SEC.1
  • $3 milliona year: the average cost of running a family office, and $6.6 million above $1 billion in assets.7
  • $1.6 billionthe average net worth of the families in J.P. Morgan’s 2026 survey of 333 offices.7

Planning arrives late.

Only 32% of American adults have a will. The share climbs with age: 46% in their 60s, 66% in their 70s and about eight in ten after 80. Most families write their plans down decades after they first needed them.8

The cost is not only on paper: 69% of Americans say financial uncertainty has made them feel depressed and anxious.14

Why now

The great wealth transfer is underway.

Cerulli Associates estimates that $124 trillion will pass between American households through 2048.15 How well it goes depends on the records, the plans and the conversations above.

  • $54 trillionis expected to pass first between spouses, more than 95% of it to women.16
  • 2%of households, the wealthiest, account for more than half of the total.15
  • $36 trillionVisa’s lower estimate of what baby boomers will pass to their heirs over 20 years, after debt, spending, taxes and fees, and leaving out the top 1%.17

Split of the $124 trillion: Cerulli Associates, 2024; the parts are rounded.15
